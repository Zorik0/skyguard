01 · The Problem

Every anomaly is two different problems
wearing the same clothes

Failure mode A

Call real weather a fault

You "correct" a genuine heatwave out of the record. The archive now says something that never happened — and climate records are read for decades.

The original measurement is gone. No later improvement to the model can recover it.

Failure mode B

Publish a broken probe

61.4 °C flows into every forecast, flood model, agricultural advisory and insurance calculation downstream of you.

Nobody downstream can tell. The number looks exactly like a measurement.

A platform that treats these the same is worse than no platform at all — it either erases real weather or ships broken data, and does so with the authority of an automated system.

03 · Approach

Four stages, and every one of them explains itself

STAGE 01
Detect
Seven statistical tests per channel. Z-score against a 90-minute trailing baseline, rate limits, frozen channels, variance steps, drift, range.
STAGE 02
Explain
Physics rules, neighbour comparison and propagation search. Weather or hardware — decided by weighted, named evidence.
STAGE 03
Correct
Five independent estimators combined, with a prediction interval from their disagreement. The raw value is never touched.
STAGE 04
Act
Anomalies correlate into incidents, incidents raise alerts and maintenance tasks, every decision lands in an audit log.
No black box

The score is a weighted sum of named tests, and the tests travel with it into the interface.

No certainty

Confidence can never reach 100%. Balanced evidence returns indeterminate, not a coin flip.

No destruction

A correction is an additional record. The raw export still contains the fault.

Approach04
04 · Stage One

Detection — measuring "unusual" in units of usual

z = |value − baseline mean| ──────────────────── baseline σ z = 1 entirely ordinary z = 2 uncommon ≈ 5% z = 3 rare ≈ 0.3% z = 4 the alarm point

Unit-free, so one threshold serves °C, hPa and m/s alike — and it adapts automatically. The same 2 °C move is alarming on a calm channel and unremarkable on a volatile one.

The detail that decides whether it works

A six-minute guard band

├──── baseline: 90 min ────┤├guard┤│ ↑ sample under test

The baseline ends six minutes before the sample. A fault that ramps up would otherwise contaminate its own reference window, dragging the baseline toward the fault and shrinking the departure being measured.

This is called leakage, and it is one of the commonest ways to build a detector that quietly misses the events it was written for.

Spike / drop

Far from its own recent average

Rate of change

Faster than air can move

Frozen

Bit-identical — a dead channel

Noise · Drift · Range

Variance step, persistent offset, impossible value

Why the distinction has force

A 5σ departure is unusual — and unusual things happen. A temperature that rose 40 °C in one minute is not unusual; there is no mechanism. Impossible is close to proof.

Rules that pass are reported too

"We checked six things, two failed, here they are" is an account an operator can argue with. "Anomaly detected" cannot be argued with — which means it also cannot be trusted.

Physics Validation06
06 · The Decisive Evidence

Weather travels. A broken probe does not.

A station cannot audit its own thermometer — it has no second opinion. Eleven other stations do.

for each relevant neighbour: for lag in −180min … +180min: did it move the same direction by at least half the magnitude? keep the match with smallest |lag|

Two matches at plausible lags, in geographic order, is strong evidence of a real event. Zero matches — nobody, anywhere, at any lag — is strong evidence of a broken probe.

Proximity is a poor guide

A peak 8 km away is a worse reference than a valley town 60 km away. Relevance blends four factors:

Classification08
08 · Why It Is Trustworthy

"94% confident" is arithmetic, not a prediction

confidence = winning weight ÷ total weight

Not a probability from a model — a division over a list the operator can read line by line and disagree with. That is the difference between a system people trust and one they eventually switch off.

Design decision 01

Both sides start at 0.3

A non-zero prior on the losing side mathematically caps the winner below 100%. The system is structurally incapable of claiming certainty about the physical world.

Design decision 02

Below 60% → indeterminate

"I don't know, a human should look" is a legitimate output, and far more useful than a confident coin flip that nobody can identify afterwards.

Design decision 03

Some findings short-circuit

A channel with zero variance, or a value outside what the instrument can produce, is not measuring the atmosphere at all. There is no weather hypothesis to weigh.

Worked example · AWS-007
raw 61.40 °C ← never modified physics 22.10 ml 22.40 neighbours 22.00 external 21.80 history 22.30 ───────────────────── corrected 22.16 °C interval 21.9 – 22.5 confidence 91%

Weights are renormalised over whichever estimators returned a value, so a missing external model widens the interval honestly rather than quietly biasing the answer.

The rule that matters most in the entire codebase: the raw value is never overwritten. Overwriting destroys the only evidence the instrument was failing, makes the archive unauditable, and means a better model can never be applied to old data — because its input is gone.

Corrected Values10
10 · Building a Fake World Honestly

Simulate the cause, not the symptom

Stage 1 — the truth

One regional atmosphere

A single shared field, sampled by all twelve stations and adjusted per site for elevation, terrain and distance from the ocean.

Cold fronts, heat waves and rainfall are applied here.

Stage 2 — the measurement

Imperfect hardware

Each station observes that truth through real instruments: noise, calibration offsets, packet loss, battery decline.

Faults are applied here, and nowhere else.

The consequence: the properties the classifier relies on are never coded — they emerge.

A front automatically appears at every station in geographic order, because they all sample the same moving field. A spike automatically appears at exactly one station, because it was added after the field was sampled. Neither behaviour is written down anywhere — which is the only reason the detection code is genuinely under test rather than being told the answer.

Deterministic

Nothing calls Math.random(). Every value is a pure function of a seed and a timestamp.

Geometric fronts

Arrival time is a projection onto the direction of travel, at 46 km/h.

Real transect

Coast → Coast Range → valley → Cascades → high desert. 8 m to 1450 m.

11 · Architecture

One pure function. Fifteen screens.
No two of them can disagree.

buildWorld({ seed, events, clock }) → World → every page reads from it

The usual way to build a dashboard gives each page its own fetching and its own state. It works right up until two pages disagree — and then the bug exists only in the gap between them. SkyGuard has exactly one such gap: none.

Inject a fault at AWS-007 and it appears on the overview, the map, the health matrix, the alert list, the maintenance queue and the audit log in the same render — because they are all reading the same array.

12 · Evaluation

Six methods, scored against ground truth

"Accuracy" is a trap here. Faults occupy perhaps 40 minutes of a 1440-minute day, so a detector that reports "no fault, ever" scores 97% accurate and is completely useless.

PRECISION

When I raise an alarm, how often am I right?

Low → operators stop trusting the alerts.

RECALL

Of the real faults, how many did I catch?

Low → broken data reaches whoever depends on it.

MethodCharacter
Rule engineFires only on the impossible
Statistical z-scoreThe classic first line
Rolling deviationRobust — MAD, not σ
Isolation Forest32 trees, unsupervised
Digital-twin disagreementDistance from expectation
Ensemble (production)All of the above, with a neighbour veto
Shadow deployment

v1.5-beta runs on every live request; its output goes nowhere near the operator. Weeks of real evidence, zero blast radius.

The honest caveat: these scores exist only because a simulator knows which minutes were faults. Real hardware never comes with that label — which is precisely why the production path publishes evidence and rules rather than a score alone. Those can be checked by a human on data that has no ground truth at all.

Performance work worth naming

Prefix-sum rolling statistics

Rolling mean and σ over every 90-minute window in O(n) instead of O(n × window) — three subtractions per query, regardless of window size.

Two-layer memoisation

React's useMemo plus a module-level LRU keyed on the exact inputs — correct only because buildWorld is a pure function.

Exact run-length flatness

Frozen channels are found by exact run length, not a variance threshold — prefix-sum variance of a constant series loses its significant digits to cancellation.

15 · What Transfers

Eight ideas worth taking to any project

  • Show your working. Every verdict carries the evidence that produced it.
  • Never destroy the original. Corrections sit beside raw data, not on top of it.
  • Refuse to be certain. No confidence reaches 100%; "indeterminate" is a valid answer.
  • One source of truth. Screens reading the same object cannot disagree.
  • Determinism by default. A bug you can reproduce is a bug you can fix.
  • Label everything. Live or simulated, on every number, always.
  • Explain the threshold, not just the result. "4.2 °C above the 90-minute baseline (7.1σ)" beats "anomaly detected".
  • Simulate the cause, not the symptom. Then your detector is genuinely under test.
Stated plainly, because the questions will come

The station network is fictional — nothing connects to real hardware. Model Lab scores exist only because a simulator knows the ground truth. Failure risk is a deterministic score, not a trained model. The operator assistant is deliberately not a language model: every answer is assembled from the same objects the screens render, so it cannot invent a number.
